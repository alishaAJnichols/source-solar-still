Why this exists

Clean water shouldn't depend on a supply chain

Source is a tool for designing your own solar water still — sized to your actual household, climate, and space. No subscriptions, no proprietary parts.

The bigger idea: knowledge like this should circulate locally, not stay locked behind a single seller. Build one, learn from it, and what you figure out helps the next person too.

⚠️ Before you drink it: distillation removes minerals, salts, heavy metals, nitrates, and kills bacteria and most pathogens very effectively. It does not reliably remove certain volatile chemicals (some pesticides, solvents, fuel residues) — these can evaporate along with the water and re-condense right back into your "clean" output. This mostly matters for well or surface water with a known contamination risk. Full details, testing guidance, and storage safety in the Learn section below.

Before you build, watch your actual site for a full day if you can. In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing spots get the most consistent sun. Avoid anywhere shaded by trees, roof overhangs, or buildings during 10am–2pm — that's when solar intensity peaks and matters most.

Shade patterns shift with the seasons. A spot that's fully sunny in summer can fall into shadow in winter as the sun's angle drops lower in the sky — check both if you're building something permanent.

Ground needs to be flat and stable — if the basin sits crooked, water won't pool correctly toward the collection edge.

Raise it slightly above grade so rain runoff doesn't pool underneath and undermine the base. Keep it away from anything flammable, and close enough to where you'll store the water that you're not hauling it far.

Watch wind exposure — too much wind cools the glass and can reduce condensation. A partial windbreak (fence, wall) helps without blocking the sun. If you keep livestock, fence it off or place it out of reach so animals don't damage the glass.

Two tools you'll lean on most: a caulk gun (for sealing every joint) and a power drill (for the frame). Both are safe and quick to learn with a little guided practice — a few solid video walkthroughs:

  • How to use a caulk gun — beginner's guide
  • Caulking gun tips, including the built-in cutter/poker
  • Power drill basics for beginners

General safety: always wear eye protection, keep hands clear of the bit, drill a pilot hole before driving screws into wood, and practice your caulk line on cardboard before doing the real seams.

Silicone sealant: 5–10 years before it dries, cracks, or loses grip, depending on UV and heat exposure. Check it yearly.

Polycarbonate glazing: ~10 years before the UV coating degrades and it starts yellowing; tempered glass lasts much longer but is more fragile.

PEX or silicone tubing: 5+ years — inspect yearly for cracking or stiffness.

HDPE basin liner: 5–10 years; metal basins can last decades but may need repainting to keep the black heat-absorbing surface.

Rigid foam insulation: effectively indefinite unless physically damaged or waterlogged.

By water source: rainwater just needs a mesh screen at the inlet to keep debris out. Tap water needs nothing extra. Brackish/saltwater is the one that changes things — salt doesn't evaporate, it crusts on the basin as water leaves. Rinse the basin regularly or add a small overflow so it never fully dries out; a hardened salt crust drags down efficiency over time.

Is collecting rainwater legal where you are? In the US, yes almost everywhere — rainwater harvesting is legal in all 50 states, with only Colorado, Utah, and Nevada having any volume limits or registration requirements (Colorado caps it at 110 gallons from rooftop collection). Outside the US, rules vary a lot by country — worth a quick local check before building a large system.

Wind: cools the glass (which hurts condensation) and physically stresses lightweight glazing. Use a partial windbreak — a fence or wall that blocks wind without blocking sun — and make sure glazing is mechanically fastened, not just resting in a frame groove.

Rain: the angled glass sheds it fine, but the collection tube's outlet should be covered or one-way so rain can't get in and dilute your stored water.

Freezing/snow: evaporation basically stops below freezing, so in real winters this is seasonal equipment. Water left sitting in the basin or tubing overnight can freeze, expand, and crack them — drain it before hard freezes.

Extreme heat: generally good for output, but check that the basin doesn't run dry — a dry basin in high heat can warp plastic components.

Mounting: given the wind risk, it should be secured, not just resting. Sawhorse-style legs are fine as a support structure, but the frame needs to be actually fastened to them, and the legs anchored — ground stakes or weighted feet at minimum, especially in windy areas. A lightweight glazed box on unanchored legs is a real wind-catch risk.

Hail: mostly a glazing-material question — polycarbonate handles impact far better than tempered glass or acrylic (see the hail question in the wizard, which adjusts the recommendation for you). But severity matters: pea-sized hail is a non-issue for any of these materials, while golf-ball-plus hail can dent even metal framing. For the worst storms, a removable cover — a simple mesh screen or hinged board you throw over it ahead of a severe weather warning — is the realistic answer, not just picking tougher glazing and hoping.

Tornadoes — an honest limit, not a design fix: no backyard structure like this is tornado-safe, the same way a garden shed isn't. Anchoring helps against strong straight-line wind, but it won't make a lightweight glazed box survive a direct hit, and no glazing choice changes that. The real answer in tornado-prone regions is building it removable: glazing that lifts off cleanly so it can go inside or into a shed when a warning is issued, loose components (jugs, tubing) secured or stored rather than left as potential debris, and siting it clear of trees or structures that could fall on it — that's a bigger risk than the wind itself for something this size.

What it removes well: dissolved minerals and salts, heavy metals, nitrates, fluoride, and other dissolved solids. The boiling/evaporation process also kills bacteria and inactivates most pathogens — this is one of the oldest, most reliable purification methods that exists.

What it doesn't reliably remove: certain volatile organic compounds (VOCs) — some pesticides, solvents, and fuel residues — that have boiling points close to or below water's own. These can evaporate right along with the water vapor and condense back into your "clean" output. This mostly matters if your source is well water near agricultural or industrial land, or any source with a known contamination history.

What to do about it: if you're at all unsure about your source, get it tested first — a standard well-water panel checks for this. If VOC contamination is a real risk, add an activated carbon pre-filter or post-filter; distillation and carbon filtering cover each other's blind spots.

Keep it clean: clean the basin regularly to prevent biofilm or algae buildup, especially with brackish sources where salt crust accumulates and can harbor growth.

Storage matters as much as the still: store finished water in a clean, sealed, opaque or dark container. Light plus any trace organics can let algae regrow even in water this pure.

New materials: rinse new liners, tubing, and sealant thoroughly before first use — even food-grade materials can carry manufacturing residue on the first go.

Don't cross-contaminate: keep raw input water and finished distillate physically separate. A container that touched source water shouldn't touch your clean storage vessel.

On mineral-free water: distilled water lacks the dissolved minerals found in tap or spring water. That's generally fine for a healthy adult — most people get minerals from food, not water — but if this becomes someone's primary water source long-term, especially a child's, it's worth a quick conversation with a doctor.

Tap/municipal water: already tested and treated by your utility — no need to test the source. You're distilling it here for extra purity, not safety.

Rainwater: not regulated by anyone. Worth a basic bacteria/pH check the first time, especially if your roof has older materials (some flashing, treated shingles) that could leach into runoff.

Well, surface, or brackish water: this is on you entirely — private wells aren't covered by EPA drinking-water rules, so nobody's testing it but you. Test at least once a year for total coliform bacteria, E. coli, nitrates, pH, and TDS at minimum. Run a broader panel (arsenic, lead, VOCs, pesticides) as a baseline the first time you use a new source, then every 3–5 years after.

Test immediately, not on schedule, if: taste, smell, or color changes; after flooding, a well repair, or new construction/agriculture nearby; before using the water for an infant, or anyone pregnant, elderly, or immunocompromised.

Home kits vs. certified labs: a $20–100 home strip kit is fine for quick pH/hardness/TDS spot checks. For anything a real health decision hinges on — bacteria, arsenic, lead, nitrates — use a state-certified lab ($100–500). Your state or county health department keeps a list of certified labs.

Testing your output, not just your source: a cheap TDS meter (around $10–15) is a genuinely useful habit — distilled water should read close to 0 ppm. If your output reads high, something's off: a cracked seal letting untreated water bypass the process, or a contaminated collection path. This is the fastest way to catch a problem with the still itself, not just the source water.

The national baseline: the International Residential Code (adopted in 49 of 50 US states) exempts one-story detached accessory structures from needing a building permit when the floor area is 200 sq ft or less.

But it varies a lot locally — about half of jurisdictions are stricter than that baseline. Common thresholds you'll actually run into: 100, 120, 144, or 200 sq ft depending on your city or county. Some real examples: Madison, WI uses 150 sq ft; New York City caps at 120 sq ft; Santa Cruz County, CA uses 120 sq ft.

Beyond square footage, three other things matter: setbacks (typically 5–10 ft from side property lines, 5–15 ft from the rear — usually the more common issue than square footage on a smaller lot), lot coverage limits (many jurisdictions cap total structure footprint — house plus every accessory structure — at 30–50% of the lot), and height (usually capped around 12–15 ft, rarely an issue for something this low-profile).

The practical answer: staying under 100–120 sq ft total footprint is the safest bet to avoid needing a permit almost anywhere in the US. Above that, it's genuinely local — a phone call to your building department is the only reliable way to know for your actual address. This matters most if your design split into several units (large household or livestock, or a size that exceeded the practical single-unit build limit) — the units add up to a total footprint faster than it might seem.
